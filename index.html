Model

Beats are found the Pan-Tompkins way: differentiate the band-passed trace, square it, integrate over a 150 ms window, then take an adaptive threshold with a 200 ms refractory period. Variability metrics follow from the RR intervals and want about a minute of clean trace before they settle.

Events are classified by shape. A blink is a fast bipolar spike under about 400 ms; a saccade is a step that holds its new level. Direction follows electrode polarity, so if left and right come out swapped, swap IN+ and IN− rather than changing anything here.

Band powers come from the same FFT feeding the spectrum. The honest test: sit still with eyes open, then close them. Occipital alpha should climb within a couple of seconds. If it doesn't, the electrodes or the reference are the problem, not the maths.

Neurofeedback the rain grows quiet as you relax

Alpha waves (8–13 Hz) grow when the brain idles calmly, most of all with the eyes closed. Here they drive a sound: as alpha rises, the rain softens and darkens, and a bell rings for every 5 seconds you hold it. Learning to quiet the rain is the training. The first 30 seconds measure your own baseline, so sit as you will during the session.

Alpha is strongest at the back of the head: IN+ on the back of the skull (part the hair, use gel), IN− behind the ear, REF on the other ear. The forehead also works, but it picks up less. Blinks and a clenched jaw are skipped automatically. On iPhone, the silent switch mutes the sound. This is relaxation practice, not treatment.

Photosensitivity warning

The targets below flicker between roughly 8 and 15 Hz, which is within the band that can provoke seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy — around one person in four thousand. Do not use this if you have a history of seizures or if anyone nearby does. Stop immediately if you feel dizzy, nauseous, disoriented, or notice any twitching. Keep the room lit, sit back from the screen, and do not run it for long stretches.

Drowsiness blink dynamics from a forehead electrode

Electrodes go on the forehead, above the eyebrows — no hair, no gel in your scalp. What you get there is dominated by eye movement rather than cortical rhythm, and for this purpose that is an advantage: the clinical standard for sleepiness, PERCLOS, is built on eyelid behaviour, not brain waves.

Three signs are tracked. Blinks get longer as you tire, they cluster into bursts, and the theta/alpha ratio creeps up. Long slow blinks are the earliest and most reliable of the three.

The score is relative to your own alert baseline, not an absolute scale — blink rate varies several-fold between people, so a raw number would mean nothing without that reference. Set the baseline when you are genuinely awake, ideally early in a session. Screen work suppresses blinking on its own, so expect the rate to read low whenever you are concentrating hard; that is why blink duration carries more weight here than blink count.

Focus time blink suppression during visual attention

Attention to a visual task suppresses blinking — a well-established effect, and the same measurement that drives the drowsiness card, read the other way round. Blinks also cluster at natural breakpoints: the end of a paragraph, a finished thought. Long gaps between them mark sustained engagement.

This counts visual engagement, not concentration in general. Thinking with your eyes closed, or working away from a screen, reads as idle here. Dry air suppresses blinking and tired eyes increase it, so compare a session to your own others rather than to anyone else's.

Tiredness and focus both act on blink rate and cannot be fully separated from it. When the drowsiness card above is scoring high, this one will understate your focus and will say so rather than quietly report a low number.

Rhythm check 30-second single-lead ECG, read beat by beat

Chest pain, fainting, sudden breathlessness, or a racing heart that won't settle? Call 103 or 112 now. Don't wait for a recording.

Press start, then you have 5 seconds to sit back and rest your arms on your legs or a table. A beep starts the recording and two beeps end it. In between, don't talk and don't move for 30 seconds. Lead I gives the clearest picture: IN+ on the left wrist or forearm, IN− on the right, REF on a bony spot such as the right ankle. The check reads the rhythm (regular, irregular, extra beats, too fast or too slow) and measures the beat's main intervals.

    Axon is not a medical device. A single lead can't rule heart disease in or out, and it cannot detect a heart attack. Treat anything flagged here as a reason to see a doctor and get a 12-lead ECG, never as a diagnosis. A clean result doesn't mean your heart is healthy, either.

    Respiration from ECG amplitude modulation and sinus arrhythmia

    Breathing moves the chest, which tilts the heart relative to the electrodes, so R-wave height rises and falls with the breath. Rate is modulated too: the heart speeds up on inhalation and slows on exhalation. Both are derived from the same ECG, with no second sensor.

    Sinus arrhythmia is the default because it is the more robust of the two. R-wave amplitude also responds to electrode drift, posture shifts and cable movement, any of which can be larger than the roughly 15% respiratory swing — so use the amplitude method as a cross-check rather than a primary reading.

    The two methods rest on different physics — one on electrode geometry, one on vagal control of the sinus node — so when they agree the estimate is trustworthy, and when they diverge something is off: usually movement artefact, or a breath rate outside the 0.1–0.6 Hz band either can resolve. RSA amplitude falls with age and rises with fitness; it is a real parasympathetic measure, but a noisy one over short windows.

    Gaze control two-channel EOG · calibrated selection menu

    The eye is a standing dipole — cornea positive, retina negative — so rotating it swings the potential at electrodes on either side. CH1 is horizontal: one electrode at the outer corner of each eye. That single channel gives a working two-item menu on its own.

    A second Pill adds CH2 vertical — above the brow and below the lower lid of one eye — which brings the menu to four items and lets blinks be told apart from a genuine look upward. The axis mode below follows your channel count automatically.

    Eye position is nearly DC, so the trace drifts back toward centre within a few seconds of holding a gaze — that is the amplifier's high-pass, not your eyes. The decoder therefore works on the step at the moment of the saccade rather than the absolute level, which is why a selection is made shortly after you move your eyes and not while you hold them still. Blinks are detected and suppressed so they do not register as an upward look.

    Wiring more than one channel. Every Pill shares the same 3V3 and GND, and every REF goes to the same bony landmark. Separate references per Pill make the lanes fight each other with common-mode noise. Outputs go to GPIO4–7, all on ADC1.

    Baud. Four channels at 500 SPS is roughly 10 kB/s, too close to 115200 for comfort — the multi-channel sketch uses 460800, so set the same here before connecting.

    What the classifier is. Five features per channel — mean absolute value, waveform length, zero crossings, slope sign changes, RMS — computed over 512 ms windows, z-scored against your training set, then matched with distance-weighted 3-nearest-neighbours. It is the standard baseline in prosthetics EMG work, and it trains from about thirty examples, which is why it suits a browser tab rather than a neural network.
